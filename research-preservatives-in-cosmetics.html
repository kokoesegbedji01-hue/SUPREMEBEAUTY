Preservatives: Why Are They in Cosmetics?

Key takeaways

  • Products containing water and organic ingredients need protection against microbial contamination.
  • Preservation can be chemical, physical, or a mix, including pH and water-activity control.
  • There is a trade-off: higher preservative levels work better but may be harder on consumers; too low may allow microbial resistance.

What it is and why it is used

Cosmetics that contain water and organic compounds can grow microbes. Preservation protects consumers from potentially pathogenic organisms and extends shelf life.

What the evidence says

A review of cosmetic preservation describes antimicrobial agents (synthetic or natural) as the most common strategy, alongside controlling water activity and pH and using multifunctional ingredients. It describes validation as a combination of good manufacturing practices, raw-material control, and challenge testing. It also notes that preservatives can have toxic effects, that high concentrations are more effective but potentially toxic for consumers, and that low concentrations can allow microbial resistance to develop.

What the evidence does not establish

This review is general. It does not conclude that any single preservative is unsafe at cosmetic use levels, and it does not support the idea that a product can skip preservation altogether. "Preservative-free" marketing deserves a question: what is protecting the formula instead?

Who may want to pay attention

People with known preservative allergies should read ingredient lists and consult a dermatologist about patch testing.

Sources

  1. Halla N, Fernandes IP, Heleno SA, et al. Cosmetics preservation: a review on present strategies. Molecules. 2018;23(7):1571. doi:10.3390/molecules23071571 Source
