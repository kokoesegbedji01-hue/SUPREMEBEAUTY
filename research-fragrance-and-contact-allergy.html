Fragrance in Beauty Products: What the Research Says About Allergy

Key takeaways

  • Fragrance is a common cause of allergic contact dermatitis, but most people are not allergic.
  • Contact allergy to fragrance is estimated at roughly 0.7% to 2.6% of the general population, and higher among people referred for patch testing.
  • "Fragrance-free" products may still contain covert fragrances.

What it is and why it is used

Fragrance materials are used across personal care products, cosmetics, and cleaning products. Many individual fragrance chemicals can act as contact sensitizers.

What the evidence says

A 2024 clinical review reports that general-population prevalence of fragrance contact allergy is between 0.7% and 2.6%, and that studies of patients referred for patch testing show 5% to 25%. Common patch-test screening tools include fragrance mix I, fragrance mix II, and balsam of Peru. The review also cautions that avoidance is difficult and that products labeled "fragrance free" may contain covert fragrances.

What the evidence does not establish

Prevalence figures describe allergy, not toxicity for everyone. This review does not address other health effects, and it does not rank products or specific fragrance chemicals for people without an allergy.

Who may want to avoid it

People with confirmed fragrance allergy, or recurring rashes after using scented products, may benefit from patch testing and a dermatologist's guidance.

Sources

  1. Rodriguez I, Wolkov M, Herbst J, et al. A whiff of trouble: navigating allergic contact dermatitis to fragrance. Cutis. 2024;114(2):41–45. doi:10.12788/cutis.1070 Source
